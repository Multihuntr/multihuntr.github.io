How delicious an idea that we can understand reality through numbers.

You are a young smarty-pants who wants to think only true things. You find out about this wonderful idea of numbers. You can count with them, you can do operations with them. When other people do the same operations, they get the same result. You can use the numbers to make simple and accurate descriptions of the world. 

It is evident that numbers are inherently true. If each person spends $12, then the total spent by 23 people is $276. This is as objective a reality as your own body and mind. Maybe even more so! And so you think to yourself:

_If we can just explain something in numbers, then we will necessarily be describing only true things!_

How sweet the taste. The allure that if we just find the right way to do the numbers, we can find the objective facts about anything. 

You grow up and you learn more sophisticated numerical techniques. You find out that objects move according to fixed physical laws. That our world runs on money, which is basically just numbers. You hear about human scientific studies which explain some proportion of the mysteries inside our body.

You begin to become very certain of things. You see all this evidence that numbers tell us about objective reality. You start to believe that numbers will guide you to only objectively true things. All is well. You can be confident because you have the combined weight of all intellectuals in history that have all bowed to the altar of numerical supremacy. Numbers are true; I am using numbers; so I am correct.

How grand it is that we can be so certain about at least these things! There's a huge world of unknowns, but at least these numbers, we cannot doubt. They are so unambiguously themselves. They are beautiful in simplicity, and powerful in function. 

But, there's something that doesn't quite make sense. If you are objectively correct, why do people sometimes disagree with you? Either you must be correct, or they are. You know that even smart people can be wrong, so you look into the numbers. Every time you look, the numbers referenced don't quite align with reality. The numbers don't quite say what they say they do, or there is some reason why these numbers don't quite capture the reality you were talking about. If they just saw the numbers the right way, then they would have to acknowledge that you are correct. Numbers are, after all, objective.

How righteous we are, standing against this tide of ignorance. With our objective reality in hand: our numbers, with which we can be certain that we are the ones who are correct. All one needs to do is dig deep enough and they will understand as we do.

But there's still a problem. Why do *you* sometimes change your mind? The same numbers which you found convincing before turn out to not be what you thought. New knowledge was gained, and now we - the ones who seek truth - have realised that those numbers weren't the full story. They didn't quite align with reality. So, now we change our understanding of what those numbers mean. Somehow, you were misled, even though you followed the numbers.

How could this be? Are numbers not objective? No, numbers are objective. They must be. We can all take the measurements ourselves and check. So, what has happened? Well, it wasn't the numbers, per se, that misled you, but the story the numbers told. The wrong numbers were collected. But that's ok! The numbers, messy and confusing, are just a part of science and rationality. The numbers that were collected were just the best guess at the time. It's hardly the fault of the investigators that they were not omniscient. This is how we learn about the world. That's all it was. 

How amazing this self-correcting process. That we can go from incorrect numbers to correct numbers through more thorough investigation! We may never be certain, but at least we have a guess which best fits the evidence. The exact guess may change, but surely, at least, the current best guess is objective. Right? Please.

You study mathematics and become a computer scientist. You read scientific papers. You make algorithms to improve a metric. You see some academic disagreements. You find yourself agreeing with some parts of each side. You find yourself disagreeing a lot, too. Even though everyone is ostensibly on the same side, we disagree about the mechanisms involved. But at least we can agree that improving the metric is a good thing, right? But papers reference multiple metrics. What happens if a model is better on only one metric? Now there is disagreement about the metric. And worse, there's a lot of evidence that the data itself is biased, especially in small datasets. It is systematically incomplete and, as far as training a model is concerned, it describes a world that does not exist. 

From this inaccurate picture of the real world, we optimise knowledgeless models towards meaningless metrics. And from these tea leaves we hope to glean the future. We are dazzled by potential, and remain optimistic that it is meaningful. This is how it works in all fields of study, right? Incomplete data lays many trails of breadcrumbs that we follow to eventually find the truth.

How beautiful it is that we all are contributing to the investigation. We argue and we think and we push the edge of knowledge inexorably outwards to uncover more and more. We create more and more powerful techniques so that we can explain more and more of the world. ... Right? Why else do we do what we do?

You've now read hundreds of papers using small datasets, applying machine learning and deep learning to them, and boldly declaring the results meaningful. It is now trivial for researchers to throw a DL algorithm at a problem and get a higher metric score. There are countless papers doing exactly, and only, that. Underlying this approach is the assumption that a higher metric score is intrinsically meaningful. And yet, each paper finds a different algorithm works best. What meaning is there to be made when the mechanisms are invisible and the conclusions are contradictory? Surely the numbers must mean something, right?

Oh, how sweet the allure. Objective truth and certainty are just outside your grasp, if only you could see the right way to think about the numbers. How tantalising that we could be unambiguously right with the path forward clear. How great it will be when we eventually figure it out, and we can know, for sure, that your way of understanding the numbers is the right one. 

Numbers don't lie to us, but our minds certainly do. Sometimes a number is just a number. We can sit around a campfire making up stories about the number, but the fact that we are discussing a number doesn't make those stories real. No matter how appealing that might be.
